## Abstract

This note examines how order pickers in large fulfillment warehouses come to understand the routing algorithm that dictates the sequence and path of their picks, and how that understanding becomes a shared resource on the floor. Pickers rarely receive an explanation of why the handheld terminal sends them where it does; they are simply directed, item by item, along a route the system computes to optimize throughput. Yet workers do not experience this as a black box they passively obey. Through interviews and floor observation across two distribution centers, we document how pickers actively probe the router: testing whether walking ahead to a known location beats the directed path, noticing when the system clusters picks to reward speed, and inferring how it trades travel distance against congestion and deadline pressure. These inferences accrue into folk heuristics that circulate informally between shifts and are taught to new hires faster than any official training conveys. We argue that this constitutes a form of triadic coordination: pickers coordinate with each other and with the routing system as an active third party by negotiating a shared interpretation of how it behaves and what it rewards. The practical stakes are real, because a picker who correctly anticipates the router conserves energy and protects their rate, while one who misreads it falls behind and draws management scrutiny. We show that the accuracy of a shared heuristic matters less than its circulation, since a widely held belief about the router reshapes collective movement on the floor whether or not it is strictly correct. The note closes by arguing that worker-side inference of algorithmic management deserves study as a coordination practice in its own right, and by proposing methods for eliciting these heuristics without distorting them, with implications for audits conducted from the worker's perspective rather than the warehouse operator's.
