## Abstract

When the system that assigns work is invisible, workers do not simply comply with it; they theorize it. This note reports on how last-mile delivery couriers build and circulate folk models of the dispatch algorithm that routes orders to them, and how those models become a medium of coordination in their own right. Drawing on interviews and shift observation with couriers across two metro markets, we trace the informal knowledge that accumulates around questions the platform never answers: why one driver gets the lucrative double order and another waits, whether declining a job hurts future offers, how the app weights distance against acceptance rate, and when it pays to reposition before a dinner rush. None of this is documented. It is inferred, tested, argued over in group chats, and passed to newcomers as practical lore. We show that these folk models are not merely coping strategies but a form of triadic coordination: couriers coordinate with one another, and with the dispatch system as a third party, by negotiating a shared interpretation of how the algorithm behaves. The accuracy of the model matters less than its circulation; a widely held belief about the algorithm reshapes collective behavior whether or not it is correct, which in turn changes what the algorithm sees and rewards. We argue this feedback loop is central to understanding algorithmic management in platform work, and that worker voice in these settings often takes the form of contesting the interpretation of the system rather than the system itself. The note closes by proposing folk models as a unit of analysis for studying algorithmacy at work: portable, observable, and revealing of the gap between how a system is designed and how it is actually understood by the people who must coordinate through it every day. We outline methods for eliciting these models without distorting them, and discuss what they imply for audits conducted from the worker's perspective rather than the platform's.
