## Abstract

From Sediment to Algorithm: Rethinking Environmental Governance through Algorithmacy in South Africa's Breede River Catchment

Critical cultural research has long examined technologies, platforms, and digital infrastructures as sites of power and meaning-making. However, environmental governance increasingly depends on algorithmic systems that do more than organise information; they participate in shaping ecological knowledge, institutional decisions, and the relationships between humans and more-than-human worlds. This paper brings the concept of algorithmacy into dialogue with environmental humanities by asking how algorithmic systems function as cultural and political actors alongside the material agency of sediments.
Drawing on ethnographic research conducted in South Africa's Breede River catchment, the paper examines how sediment moves beyond its conventional representation as inert geological matter to become an active participant in environmental governance, conservation practices, and multispecies relations. At the same time, decisions about rivers are increasingly informed by digital infrastructures, including environmental databases, monitoring technologies, GIS mapping, predictive models, and data-driven governance platforms, that algorithmically organise what counts as environmental evidence and legitimate knowledge.
Rather than treating these digital systems as neutral tools, I argue that they operate as algorithmic third parties that coordinate interactions among state institutions, conservation authorities, scientists, and local communities. These systems prioritise particular forms of evidence, render some environmental concerns more visible than others, and shape whose ecological knowledge is recognised within governance processes. Algorithmacy therefore becomes a useful conceptual lens for understanding how environmental coordination increasingly occurs through algorithmic mediation.
Situated within political ecology, science and technology studies, and the environmental humanities, the paper explores how material processes and algorithmic processes co-produce environmental governance. Sediment and algorithm emerge as distinct but interconnected actors: one material and ecological, the other computational and cultural. Together they shape environmental decision-making, authority, and possibilities for sustainability transformation.
Methodologically, the paper draws on ethnography, participant observation, walking methodologies, visual documentation, and secondary environmental data collected during doctoral research. By bringing algorithmacy into conversation with environmental governance, the paper contributes to critical cultural research by extending debates about algorithmic mediation beyond digital culture into the governance of ecological systems. It argues that understanding contemporary environmental justice requires attention not only to rivers, landscapes, and multispecies relations, but also to the algorithmic infrastructures that increasingly mediate how these environments are known, governed, and cared for.

## Author bios

Mistancia Kanengoni is an environmental anthropologist and environmental humanities scholar whose research examines the intersections of materiality, environmental governance, multispecies relations, and sustainability transformation in Africa. She recently completed her PhD at the University of Cape Town (UCT), South Africa, where her doctoral research explored the social life of sediments in the Breede River Catchment through an ethnographic and interdisciplinary approach.
Her research investigates how sediments, often regarded as passive geological matter, actively participate in shaping ecological processes, environmental governance, social relations, and questions of justice. Drawing on environmental anthropology, political ecology, Science and Technology Studies (STS), and more-than-human scholarship, she examines the dynamic relationships between rivers, landscapes, human communities, state institutions, conservation authorities, and non-human life. Her work challenges technocratic approaches to environmental management by demonstrating how environmental knowledge is produced through interactions between material processes, local experience, scientific practice, and governance institutions.
