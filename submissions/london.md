## Abstract

Education is where most people first coordinate with another human through an algorithmic third party, and it is where the terms of that coordination are learned without ever being taught. A student and a teacher, or an applicant and an admissions officer, increasingly meet only through an intermediary system: predicted grades, similarity scores, ranking and matching algorithms, and, in the English case of 2020, a national standardisation model. This note argues that the pedagogical consequence of that mediation has been overlooked, and that it constitutes a hidden curriculum in compliance.

The 2020 Ofqual episode offers an unusually legible case. Teachers submitted centre-assessed grades; an algorithm adjusted them with reference to each school's historical performance; students received the output. Neither of the two human parties controlled the third, and the third pursued an objective, the stability of the national grade distribution, that belonged to neither of them. The system's proxy for the individual candidate was, in substantial part, the record of the institution they attended. Where bureaucratic assessment had once offered a named official, a stated rationale, and a route of appeal, the standardisation model initially offered none of these; it inherited bureaucracy's impersonality while shedding its accountability apparatus. This suggests that algorithmacy in educational settings is not straightforwardly a novel coordination form but, in part, a bureaucratic one with its discretionary and explanatory functions removed.

The note then considers what students were positioned to learn. Absent any account of how the intermediary worked, the available strategy was optimisation rather than interrogation: subject choices, coursework, and application behaviour shaped around metrics whose objectives were unstated and unexaminable. Compliance came to function as a display of academic competence. The paper contends that the students who could articulate that the model was scoring their school rather than their work were exercising a civic and analytic capacity that no part of their formal education had developed, and that the 2020 refusal is better read as evidence of that capacity than as mere grievance.

It follows that algorithmic literacy, as a curricular aim, should be specified more demandingly than digital safety or tool proficiency. The note proposes four questions as its minimum content: what objective does this system pursue, whose interests does that objective encode, what does it use as a proxy for me, and what recourse exists. It argues that these are answerable in ordinary classroom settings.

The account is offered with two qualifications. Human assessment carries well-documented biases of its own, and the reversion to centre-assessed grades produced significant inflation and inequity. The argument advanced here is not that the model performed worse than teachers would have, but that no party to the coordination was equipped to examine it, and that this incapacity was itself an educational failure.

## Outline

- Assessment increasingly places a system between student and teacher, and the coordination that results has been examined as governance but rarely as pedagogy.
- Situates algorithmacy in educational settings, distinguishing coordination through an assessment system from direct evaluation by a teacher.
- Sets out the 2020 Ofqual standardisation model as the case: centre-assessed grades submitted by teachers, adjusted against institutional performance history, received as output by students.
- Identifies the objective mismatch, in which the system pursued distributional stability while both human parties pursued individual accuracy, and examines the use of school record as proxy for candidate.
- Compares this against bureaucratic assessment, arguing that the model retained impersonality while shedding the named official, stated rationale, and route of appeal that administrative practice had established.
- Considers the resulting hidden curriculum, in which optimisation against unexaminable metrics substitutes for interrogation of them, and compliance comes to read as competence.
- Reads the 2020 refusal as evidence of an analytic capacity that formal education had not developed, rather than as grievance.
- Proposes four questions as the minimum content of algorithmic literacy as a curricular aim, and addresses the counter-case of teacher bias and post-rollback grade inflation.
