## Abstract

The rapid development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is transforming the character animation production pipeline, creating opportunities to reduce production time, automate repetitive tasks, and expand the capabilities of individual creatives and small production teams. These opportunities may be particularly significant in the Global South, where animation practitioners often operate within resource-constrained environments. However, the increasing integration of AI into creative production also raises questions concerning authorship, creative agency, cultural representation, transparency, skill development, access, and the displacement of human labour. This paper explores the ethical integration of AI within the character animation production pipeline by examining the relationship between human creative decision-making and AI-assisted production.

The study investigates how AI tools are currently being integrated across stages of the character animation pipeline, including research and ideation, scriptwriting, character design, voice generation, rigging, in-betweening, colouring, animation, and post-production, and considers the efficiency gains associated with their use. It further examines ethical concerns surrounding AI-assisted creative production, particularly regarding authorship, ownership, cultural representation, transparency, skill displacement, and the extent to which creative decisions should remain under human control. Particular attention is given to the challenges and opportunities experienced by animation practitioners in the Global South, including access to technologies, affordability, digital infrastructure, AI literacy, and the representation of local cultures within AI systems.

The paper argues that AI should function as a creative partner that supports rather than replaces human creative agency. It proposes an ethical framework for human–AI coordination within the character animation production pipeline based on human creative oversight, transparency and disclosure, AI literacy, cultural relevance, responsible authorship, and equitable access to technology. By centring the experiences and production realities of creators in emerging economies, this research contributes a Global South perspective to discussions surrounding algorithmically mediated creative labour and proposes principles for more ethical, transparent, and human-centred AI-assisted animation production.

## Author bios

Chevonnese Chevers Whyte, MBA, Dip. Ed., B.Sc.
chevonnese.cheverswhyte@uwi.edu
Chevonnese Chevers Whyte has been a lifelong student of the creative arts.  She has facilitated creative minds since 2010 at CARIMAC, UWI, Mona.  This journey led to her research interest in animation education, where she is pursuing a PhD in  Monitoring, Measurement and Evaluation.  As a certified teacher, her current endeavours include investigating animation education in Jamaica and its impact on the development of students and teachers.  Chevonnese has also assisted with crafting the animation industry through the Jamaica Animation Nation Network (serving as its Vice President).  Her network includes the Animation Educator’s Forum, ASIFA-Hollywood, and the Society of Animation Studies.  She firmly believes that animation educators should be working in the industry, providing real-world experiences to their learners.  Her experiences stem from working on the award-winning animated series Abeeku and the maroons, an original IP for Night Vision Media Ltd., where she is co-owner.

Kevin Jackson, M.Sc., Dip. Ed., B.Sc.
kevin.jackson@uwi.edu
Kevin Jackson is an award-winning writer, director and animator. He holds a MSc. Media Management, recipient of the prestigious Silve Musgrave Award for achievement in arts. He is the former President of the Jamaica Animation Nation Network and former director of communications and Marketing for the Jamaica Film and Television Association. He is a lecturer at the University of the West Indies Mona in the Animation and Film departments.


Robert Patmore, BFA
designarts183@yahoo.com
Robert Patmore is a dedicated Visual Arts and Animation educator who has served full-time with the Ministry of Education for over 16 years and part-time with HEART Trust NTA. A graduate of the Edna Manley College of the Visual and Performing Arts, he champions the belief that creativity is central to the holistic development of life—where through the eyes of the creative, the plausible becomes possible.  
 
His research interests focus on developing innovative learning methods that engage creatives by promoting speed without losing soul, fostering connection without sacrificing communication, and increasing quantity without diminishing quality. Through writing and assessment-driven engagement strategies, he advocates for Art to be recognized as a core subject rather than an elective or hobby.  
 
As a Level 4 Assessor and active JANN member for over five years, Patmore continues to contribute to national conversations on creativity and education. He views Art as a universal language—one that bridges aesthetics and practicality, empowering the next generation to shape the world with vision, skill, and purpose.
