## Abstract

The content creator economy stages an unusual labour relation as they produce a novel figure of the worker, one that is constantly disciplined but never informed. Content creators are undocumented platform workers, and their engagement pods and loops are a nascent form of collective action conducted through the very platforms that discipline and govern them.  Content creators are rarely thought of as platform workers in the way gig workers are, yet their labour is structured by the same defining conditions of algorithmically mediated management without documentation, recourse or formal employment status. Content workers work on an infrastructure that is proprietary and black boxed, unable to know, access or direct the very logics that determine the visibility and conditions of their labour. Drawing on 26 semi-structured interviews with content creators in India across platforms such as Instagram, YouTube and Moj, conducted using a systematic questionnaire and subsequently recorded and transcribed for analysis, the paper traces how creators initially driven by self-expression are increasingly conditioned by algorithms that reward consistent engagement. The paper highlights how creators learn the lexicon of algorithm mainly through trial and error, and peer gossip; and how they respond by devising tactics that game, anticipate and accommodate its shifting rules. Engagement groups, Loops and trending Tactics emerge as grassroot strategies to subvert platform restrictions. It is argued that punitive withdrawal of visibility, what I like to call ‘algorithmic violence’, sits at the center of this labour. It produces its defining contradiction of a creativity paradox, where the very system that feeds on the creativity and affective labour of its workers,structurally starves them of the time and possibility of failure that creativity needs to survive. In locating collective action not in unions but in grassroot acts of reciprocity, the paper offers a unique worm-eye view of algorithmic management, demonstrating how digital spaces serve as both areas for creative expression and sites of structural power.  Ultimately, this work seeks to contribute to the discourse on digital labour, showing how a distinct sociality arises at the intersection of individual agency and platform logic, redefining notions of creativity, agency and community for Indian content creators. In doing so, the paper foregrounds a Global South perspective largely absent from existing platform labour literature, one situated in a region that constitutes one of the largest and fastest-growing hubs of digital consumption and, by extension, digital exploitation, yet remains largely outside the ambit of the regulatory protections that have begun to shape platform labour debates in Western contexts.

## Outline

Creators are algorithmically managed like gig workers, but without documentation or recourse.
Algorithm's rules are learned through trial and error and peer gossip, never disclosed.
Engagement pods, loops, and trending tactics = grassroots collective action, not union organizing.
"Algorithmic violence": punitive withdrawal of visibility as the core disciplinary tool.
"Creativity paradox": platforms feed on creativity while denying the time/failure it needs.
Based on 26 interviews across Instagram, YouTube, Moj — a worm's-eye view of algorithmic management.
Centers a Global South perspective missing from platform labour literature.
Traces a new sociality at the intersection of individual agency and platform logic.

## Author bios

Disha Khurana has been awarded the Senior Good Governance Fellowship by the Government of Punjab, where she is working on data-driven policy at the Department of Good Governance. She holds an M.A. in Development Studies from IIT Mandi and a Bachelors in Sociology from Hindu College, University of Delhi. As an early-career female researcher writing from within the Global South, she studies the content creator economy as both scholar and digital native — a positioning that shaped her master's thesis on the political economy of content creation and continues to inform her research on platform governance and algorithmic labour.
