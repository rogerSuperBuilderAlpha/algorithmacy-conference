## Abstract

Algorithmacy describes the competence of coordinating with another person through an algorithmic third party. Most discussion of it focuses on skills, control and governance. This paper asks a different question: what does coordinating this way cost the human nervous system? Humans evolved to coordinate face to face, in relationships where belonging, status, fairness, reciprocity and a sense of control were negotiated directly and could be read in other people's faces. When a ranking, matching or rating system stands between people, many of those signals disappear, become opaque, or are turned into metrics.

I draw together three bodies of evidence. First, organisational research on algorithmic management and platform work shows reduced autonomy, information asymmetry, isolation and lower perceived fairness of algorithmic decisions, including among workers in the Global South. Second, needs-based frameworks, such as belongingness, self-determination theory, job demand-control and effort-reward imbalance, predict that these conditions frustrate core psychological needs and harm health. Third, neuroscience links frustration of those needs to specific systems. Exclusion and unfairness engage the dorsal anterior cingulate and anterior insula. Loneliness produces threat hypervigilance. Uncontrollable stress impairs prefrontal regulation through mechanisms identified in learned-helplessness research. Chronic stress builds allostatic load. Quantified social feedback engages mesolimbic reward circuitry.

The direct evidence is thin, and I am explicit about that. No study I am aware of has measured neural change in people who coordinate through algorithms at work. I therefore propose a hypothesis rather than report a finding. Chronic, low-visibility need frustration inside triadic, algorithm-mediated coordination may build allostatic load, and over time this may reshape the networks that regulate social threat, control and reward. The predicted pattern is heightened salience and threat responding, weakened prefrontal regulation, and reward systems tuned to metric feedback rather than to relationships.

I outline a research programme to test this. It combines ecological momentary assessment with cortisol and heart-rate-variability measures in platform and algorithmically managed workers. It adds laboratory paradigms in which the excluder or allocator is an algorithm rather than a person, and longitudinal neuroimaging cohorts. I close with design implications from a Caribbean transformation practice: legibility, recourse and restored human reciprocity as ways to protect wellbeing, so that algorithmacy becomes a competence people build rather than a strain they absorb.

## Outline

- Algorithmacy as triadic coordination, and why the human cost is under-theorised
- Evolved social needs: belonging, status, fairness, reciprocity, control
- Evidence: algorithmic management frustrates these needs
- Neural correlates of need frustration: social pain, unfairness, loneliness, uncontrollable stress, reward feedback
- The hypothesis: an allostatic pathway to network-level change, and the limits of the evidence
- Research programme: EMA plus physiology, algorithm-as-agent lab paradigms, longitudinal cohorts
- Design and governance implications from a Caribbean (Global South) perspective

## Author bios

Lorraine Villaroel is AI and Transformation Lead at the Caribbean Emergence Institute in Trinidad and Tobago, a regional workforce development, innovation and emerging technology institute established by The Rose Foundation. With more than 23 years of professional experience spanning organisational operations, digital solutions and community development, she builds practical applications and AI solutions for organisations. Based in Arima, she facilitates transformation and AI-building cohorts, and helped bring the Algorithmacy Conference 2026 to Port of Spain through FutureTT, focusing on AI-mediated work and communication.
