## Abstract

Most organizations adopt AI the way they run everything else: leadership picks the tool, mandates the rollout, and measures compliance. This report describes an inverted architecture, drawn from building the Work Evolution Framework (WEF). The algorithmic third party is allowed to do exactly two things: match people to work, and route decisions to the right human. Every judgment call stays with a person, and everyone affected can see why that person was the one asked.

The mechanics rest on three moves.

First, WEF replaces job descriptions with task portfolios: outcome-defined units of work, each carrying an explicit list of required skills. When someone is hired, onboarding begins with a skill assessment: a mutual inventory of strengths, deficits, and posture toward growth. The new employee and the organization walk in holding the same map. Because work and people are profiled in the same vocabulary, matching is continuous. It doesn't stop at hiring.

Second, the same matching logic governs judgment. When a decision arises inside a task, the system doesn't score options and choose. It finds the qualified human and routes the call, with decision logs and dissent capture keeping the trail auditable. This closes a failure mode that legacy hierarchy and algorithmic management share: in one, title picks the decision-maker; in the other, the model decides. Both sever judgment from expertise.

Third, automation spreads from the bottom up because the incentive sits with the worker. WEF prices every task through an eight-factor compensation model. The two factors doing the work here are Efficiency, which pays for removing waste from the system, and Level of Task, which pays more for harder work; the other six count contributions that traditional systems miss, from teaching to invisible glue work. A contributor who automates part of their own task keeps the compensation attached to it through the transition, then moves to higher-rated work once the automation is stable. WEF calls this the automation dividend. The gains are shared, so people hunt for places to bring AI into their own workflow. Adoption happens task by task, chosen by the people closest to the work.

A governance spine holds the three moves together: published factor weights, versioned rubrics, an appeals process with documented rationale, and bias audits across demographic groups. Two boundaries are hard. Workers can inspect every algorithmic score that affects them, and behavioral surveillance never substitutes for contribution evidence. The design position is blunt: an implementation that improves efficiency while worsening fairness has failed.

The framework was developed across twenty years in product engineering and developer-relations roles at AMD, Dell, IBM, Atlassian, MongoDB, AWS, SingleStore, and Namecheap. The report walks through the mechanics, the guardrails, and the open questions: how routing holds up at scale, how assessments resist gaming, and what breaks when tasks are defined too coarsely or too finely. Other practitioners should walk away able to pilot the pattern and test it against their own context.

## Outline

- Context and time window: patterns observed across twenty years (2006–2026) in product engineering and developer relations at eight technology companies, distilled into WEF
- The governance gap this addresses: hierarchy lets title pick the decision-maker, and algorithmic management lets the model decide; both sever judgment from expertise
- Task portfolios: outcome-defined tasks with explicit required-skills lists, replacing job descriptions as the unit of organization
- Skill assessment as mutual inventory: strengths, deficits, and posture toward growth, held identically by worker and organization
- Decision routing: decision rights tied to task ownership, escalation triggered by dependency and risk, not by status, with decision logs and dissent capture
- The automation dividend: automation pays under the Efficiency factor, compensation is retained through the transition, and freed capacity moves to higher-rated work, so AI adoption becomes worker-led
- Governance boundaries: published weights, versioned rubrics, appeals, bias audits, inspectable scores, and no surveillance standing in for contribution evidence
- What others can learn: pilot-first rollout, the failure modes to watch (gamed assessments, fairness regressions, wrong task granularity), and open questions at organizational scale
