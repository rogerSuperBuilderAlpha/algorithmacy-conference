## Abstract

Artificial intelligence has historically occupied a narrow role within biotechnology companies, largely confined to discovery activities such as target identification and molecule design. Agentic AI changes that equation by bringing automation into the development organization itself. Regulatory affairs, medical writing, clinical operations, quality systems, pharmacovigilance, CMC, and program management can now incorporate AI directly into everyday work.

For small biotechnology companies, this opportunity arrives with a significant constraint. Unlike large pharmaceutical organizations, they cannot justify building proprietary AI systems, hiring dedicated AI engineering groups, or creating extensive governance structures around emerging technologies. Most must rely on commercially available platforms while working within the same regulatory environment that governs much larger companies.

This presentation explores how small biotech teams can implement agentic AI in development functions without access to enterprise-scale infrastructure. The discussion focuses on practical questions: Which activities are appropriate for delegation to agents? What information should never be exposed to external systems? How should human review be structured when outputs may influence regulated documents or development decisions? What level of documentation is sufficient to support inspection readiness? How can organizations evaluate whether an agent is creating value rather than simply generating additional work?

The presentation argues that the primary challenge is not technical. It is organizational. Small biotech companies must develop the ability to supervise AI agents, understand their limitations, and define clear boundaries around their use. Success depends less on building new technology than on adapting existing development processes to incorporate systems that can act with increasing autonomy.

As agentic AI moves beyond discovery and into the core functions responsible for advancing products toward approval, small biotechnology companies face a rare opportunity to expand organizational capacity without expanding headcount. Whether they realize that opportunity will depend on their ability to implement AI in a manner that is both practical and defensible within a regulated environment.

## Author bios

Amanda McEwen is Senior Vice President of Clinical Development and JK07 Program Lead at Salubris Biotherapeutics, with more than 16 years of experience advancing therapeutics from early development through regulatory approval. She has led development programs across multiple therapeutic areas and modalities, contributing to the approvals of Inbrija™ for Parkinson’s disease and Rivfloza™ for primary hyperoxaluria type 1. Amanda is an Executive Fellow at Harvard Business School, adjunct faculty in Northeastern University’s Regulatory Affairs program, and a PhD candidate in Business Analytics at Bentley University. She is particularly interested in the intersection of biotechnology innovation, regulatory strategy, and artificial intelligence.
