Where the day goes.

Three numbers from 2026 reports. The sources are at the bottom of the page.

A small firm doesn’t lose the day on the legal question. It loses it on intake, chasing documents, reminders, and follow-ups. Three 2026 reports, each looking at a different part of the profession, land on the same work.

  • 4 days

    Of 304 US lawyers ShareFile surveyed, about half in-house and half at firms, 47% say client intake takes four days or longer. Most think it should take two or less.1

  • 5 hours

    89% of the small-firm legal professionals AI Lawyer surveyed lose five or more hours a week to non-billable admin. Intake is the stage they name most often for errors.2

  • 33%

    For a third of small firms in Clio’s survey, finding the time is the biggest hurdle to new technology. There is no IT desk to set it up.3

A lot of those firms already use AI. Clio found that just under a third of the firms using it had increased revenue. Someone still has to set the tools up and keep them running. That’s the job.

What the agents do.

The same five jobs in most offices. The “Never” column is the limit.

Each job has a line the agent doesn’t cross, and someone who signs off. We write both down before anything is connected, and both show up in the portal.

The five chores, what the agent does, what it never does, and who signs off
Chore The agent Never A person
Intake reply Drafts a reply to a new inquiry the day it arrives, asking for the facts the firm listed and offering a time to speak with someone at the firm. Decides whether to take the matter, or quotes a fee. Approves the first reply, and every reply after that, until the firm says the pattern is right.
Document chase Notices what was asked for and not received, and sends the reminder on the day you set. Reads the document for legal meaning, or files it with a court. Sees the list of what is still missing.
Deadline reminders Repeats dates already on the firm’s calendar to the person who owns them, on the schedule the firm sets. Creates a legal deadline, or moves one. The lawyer who owns the matter. The agent does not replace the docket.
Review replies Drafts a response to a public review, in the firm’s voice. Posts the reply itself. Posts it, or tells the engineer to change the draft.
Follow-ups After a consult or a quiet file, drafts the next note and queues it. Gives legal advice in that note. Sends it. The portal shows what went out and what is waiting.

The agents work in the systems you already have. Clio, MyCase, email, and the phone stay where they are.

The first month.

About three hours of your time, in the first week.

  1. Week 1

    We learn the office

    On calls, and in the tools you already use. We write down the jobs, who does them, which systems hold the files, and what should stay manual. That list is what we agree to.

  2. Weeks 2 to 4

    The agents are set up

    That’s the $5,000. We don’t connect anything until the access is agreed in writing. You watch the first rows show up in the portal, and someone at the firm approves each one.

  3. Day 30

    A checkpoint

    A call. What is working, what should change, and whether you want the monthly work. You can stop here.

  4. After that

    Month to month

    $1,000 a month while we run the agents. Stop with 30 days’ written notice. We revoke access and give you a written list of what was connected and how it worked.

What we need from you: one person who knows the work, the access we write down together, and about three hours in the first week.

Confidentiality and access.

Written down before we connect anything. This page isn’t the contract.

A firm has to be able to put this in a vendor file. Many use ABA Formal Opinion 512, with Rule 1.6 on confidentiality and Rule 5.3 on supervising a nonlawyer who touches the work.5 We answer the firm’s vendor questions in writing before the work starts.

  • Client files stay in the systems you already use. We do not copy them. The portal keeps the record of what the agents did, including the drafts they wrote and who approved them. A draft can contain client information. That record is the firm’s, and the firm can have it deleted.
  • Your information is not used to train a model. Where a step needs a model, we name the model and the vendor first, under terms that exclude training.
  • Access is the least the chore needs. It is written down, and you can revoke it.
  • A confidentiality agreement is signed before anything is connected.
  • You can stop the agents. That switch is yours.
  • The portal is the record. What ran, what’s waiting for someone, and what was blocked.
  • We do not have a SOC 2 report. If your vendor file needs one, we will say so in writing, with who can see what.

Questions.

Why law firms first?

The chores are the same in a lot of owner-run offices: intake, documents, reminders, follow-ups. Law firms already have to think about confidentiality, so the access rules get written down properly. The next offices are dental practices and med spas, real estate offices, and CPA firms.

What if we already use Clio, MyCase, or something like them?

Keep it. The agents work with the system you have. Studio does not replace the practice software, and that subscription stays yours.

Can’t we just use ChatGPT?

You can. Paid tools run from about $20 to $500 a month, software only, by the vendors’ published prices, and someone in the office still has to run them. What happens when something breaks?

Agents miss steps, and offices change. You write it in the portal, or you say it on the monthly call. Fixing that is the monthly work. The reply time is agreed before the work starts. This page does not promise that nothing will break.

How long is this, and how do we stop?

The setup is once. The month is month to month after the day-30 checkpoint. Stop with 30 days’ written notice. The agents stop, the access is revoked, and you get a written list of what was connected and how it worked.

Can the staff see what the agents did?

Yes. The portal is the record: what ran, what is waiting for approval, what was blocked, and what you asked to change. You invite the people who should see it.

Who owns what gets built?

You own your files, your accounts, and the description of how your office works. The agents run on that. What happens to a specific workflow if you leave is written into the agreement, including the handover list.

Become an engineer.

How the group works, and how to join.

Every business is going to need an AI engineer. Most are too small to pay for a full-time one, which runs about $200,000 a year.

So Studio sells a fractional version. A one-time setup fee puts a business’s agents in place. A monthly fee pays for an engineer to check in on them and manage them. As a guide, that is about $5,000 to set up and $1,000 a month. A given implementation can cost more or less, but for a normal small business it should be enough.

How the group works.

Finding clients is the hard part, so we do it together. When you join, Roger sends you an API key for the shared mailbox. Each engineer runs their own Grok Bot and agents, emailing and posting on X to bring in leads, all under the Algorithmacy Studio name.

You run your own shop. A client who comes in through your outreach is your client. When you have as many as you can handle, say 50, you send new ones to other engineers in the group to manage. That is how it grows.

One engineer’s agents only reach so far. Everyone’s agents, coordinated under one brand, reach a lot further. That is the reason for the group.

What the work is.

An Algorithmacy engineer is a named person on a client account. You learn the office, set up the agents, and post a plain report after a run. Someone at the firm signs off before a new kind of email goes out.

How to join.

Open a pull request that adds your photo and two sentences under Your Engineers, and show the work the process page asks for. That pull request is the application. Access starts after it is merged and an invite is sent to the email in the pull request. The Studio owner sends the invite.
